# HOW TO EDIT
# - A line like [food.chart] starts a block. Keep block names as they are.
# - Inside a block, a line that starts with a field name and a colon starts a field:
#     title:   the heading in the side panel
#     text:    the main text
#     finding: "What the data says" (the main conclusion of a chart)
#     method:  "How we know" (methodology)
#     source:  one source per line, written as   Name of the source | https://link
#     alt:     what the pixel screen shows, read aloud by screen readers
#   A field can run over several lines: it continues until the next field or block.
# - An empty line starts a new paragraph. A line starting with "- " is a bullet.
# - **bold**, *italic* and [link text](https://...) work inside any text.
# - {name} is replaced with the sea lion's name.
# - Lines starting with # are notes like this one and never appear on the site.
# - The [lcd] block holds labels drawn on the pixel screen. The pixel font only has
#   CAPITAL LATIN LETTERS, digits and . , % - + / : ~ < > ? ! — keep labels as short

title: 47,422 sightings
text:
Before you meet one sea lion, meet all of them on record.

GBIF, the open global database of where living things have been found, holds **47,422 records** of the California sea lion: museum specimens, survey counts and, above all, phone photos. Two out of three records come from people sharing pictures on iNaturalist.
finding: People have logged more California sea lion records since 2020 (25,960) than in all the years before it (20,457).
method: All GBIF occurrence records of *Zalophus californianus* (GBIF taxon 2433458) as of 25 November 2026, counted by the year of the sighting. 1,005 records have no year and are not in the chart. The last bar covers 2020 to November 2026 and is still growing. One animal can be recorded many times, so this counts sightings, not sea lions.
source: GBIF occurrence search: Zalophus californianus | https://www.gbif.org/occurrence/search?taxon_key=2433458
alt: A big number, 47,422 records, above a bar chart of records per decade. The bar for the 2020s is by far the tallest.

[intro.map]

Each lit pixel is a patch of sea or coast about 48 km across with at least one record.

California sea lions live in the eastern North Pacific, from southeast Alaska to the Pacific coast of central Mexico. They breed from the Channel Islands off southern California to central Mexico (the orange line).
finding: The map shows where people look as much as where sea lions live. One pixel on the San Diego coast holds about 4,450 records, one in San Francisco about 3,370. San Miguel and San Nicolas, the two islands where about 90% of California's pups are born, have only a few hundred each.
method: GBIF map tiles for *Zalophus californianus* (42,428 records with coordinates and no flagged location problems fall inside this map), regridded so that one pixel is about 0.43° of latitude. The tiles update less often than the live count. Coastline: Natural Earth. We drew NOAA's description of the breeding range as a line from 34°N to 23.5°N; range and breeding range come from NOAA Fisheries, the pup share from NOAA's Channel Islands counts.

# ───────────────────────────── food ─────────────────────────────

[food.game]
title: Food: Snake
text:
{name} winds through the water like the snake in the old phone game, and grows with every bite.

California sea lions eat squid, anchovies, mackerel, rockfish and sardines, found where cold, nutrient-rich water wells up along the coast.
source: NOAA Fisheries: California sea lion | https://www.fisheries.noaa.gov/species/california-sea-lion
alt: Snake game. A sea lion head pulls a growing tail around the screen, eating seven kinds of fish and squid.

[food.chart]
title: 142 species, 7 regulars
text: Scientists learn what sea lions eat by sifting their scat for fish ear bones and squid beaks.
finding: Of 142 prey species found in sea lion scat, only seven are eaten commonly: market squid, northern anchovy, shortbelly rockfish, jack mackerel, Pacific mackerel, Pacific sardine and Pacific hake. The favorite follows the ocean: anchovy in the 1980s, market squid in the 1990s and 2000s.
method: Scat collected at three of the Channel Islands from 1981 to 2015. One square stands for one prey species; the seven filled squares are the common ones.
source: Lowry, Nehasil & Moore 2022, Marine Ecology Progress Series 692:1–21 | https://doi.org/10.3354/meps14096
alt: A grid of 142 squares with 7 filled in, labelled squid, anchovy, rockfish, jack mackerel, Pacific mackerel, sardine and hake.


# ───────────────────────────── sleep ─────────────────────────────

[sleep.game]
title: Sleep: Tetris
text:
Sleepy sea lions stack up like Tetris blocks. On shore they haul out on beaches and rocks, and along the West Coast also on marina docks, jetties and buoys.

At sea they "raft": a rafting sea lion holds its flippers above the water, motionless, to rest and to regulate its body temperature.
source: NOAA Fisheries: California sea lion | https://www.fisheries.noaa.gov/species/california-sea-lion
alt: Tetris game. Sleeping sea lions drop onto a floating dock and pile up while z's float away.

[sleep.chart]
title: The missing chart
text: We wanted to show how many hours a California sea lion sleeps. That number does not exist.
finding: We found no published study that records a California sea lion's natural sleep with brain sensors. Such studies exist for four other eared seals, among them Steller and southern sea lions.
method: We checked the 2019 review "Sleep in Aquatic Mammals" by Oleg Lyamin and Jerome Siegel and searched for newer studies. Brain recordings of stranded California sea lions do exist, but they were made under sedation to look for seizures, not to measure sleep. We chose not to fill the gap with numbers from other species.
source: Lyamin & Siegel 2019, "Sleep in Aquatic Mammals" (book chapter) | https://teams.semel.ucla.edu/sites/default/files/publications/19_aquatic_mammals_proof_lyamin_siegel.pdf
source: Williams et al. 2023, Pinniped electroencephalography, Frontiers in Veterinary Science | https://doi.org/10.3389/fvets.2023.1040125
alt: Four small eared seals, each with a check mark: sleep recorded. A California sea lion with a question mark: no data.


# ───────────────────────────── play ─────────────────────────────

[play.game]
title: Play: Pong
text:
A ball bouncing on a steady beat, and a head that meets it every time. This game belongs to Ronan, a real California sea lion.

Ronan was born in the wild in 2008. After stranding three times from malnutrition, she was declared unable to return to the ocean. Since 2010 she has lived at UC Santa Cruz, where she learned to bob her head in time to a beat, and then to music.
source: University of California: Ronan returns for an encore (2025) | https://www.universityofcalifornia.edu/news/jammin-ronan-rhythmically-trained-sea-lion-returns-encore-and-performs-well-humans
alt: Pong game. A sea lion bobs her head and hits a ball against a paddle on every beat.

[play.chart]
title: A tenth of a blink
finding: Ronan's timing varies by about 15 milliseconds from one beat to the next. A blink of a human eye takes about 150. In a 2025 study her beat-keeping was as good as or better than that of 10 student volunteers.
method: Ronan bobbed her head to a percussive beat; 10 UC Santa Cruz undergraduates moved an arm up and down to a metronome. Over 12 years she took part in about 2,000 rhythm sessions of 10 to 15 seconds each, by her own choice: she is never punished or denied food for skipping one.
source: Cook, Hood, Rouse & Reichmuth 2025, Scientific Reports | https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-025-95279-1
source: UC Santa Cruz news (2025) | https://news.ucsc.edu/2025/05/ronan-sea-lion-beats-humans/
alt: Two bars on a millisecond scale: Ronan's beat-to-beat variation, 15 ms, and a human eye blink, 150 ms.


# ───────────────────────────── talk ─────────────────────────────

[talk.game]
title: Talk: Egg catch
text:
In the old handheld game, a wolf catches eggs rolling down four ramps. Here a mother sea lion catches calls, and only one of the voices belongs to her pup.

Real mothers do this every few days. Back from sea, a female finds her pup among hundreds of others by its voice, then smells it as a final check.
source: NOAA Fisheries: California sea lion | https://www.fisheries.noaa.gov/species/california-sea-lion
alt: Egg-catch game. Calls roll down four ramps; the mother turns to catch only her own pup's call.

[talk.chart]
title: One voice in 27,146
finding: Each July scientists count the pups on San Miguel Island. Between 1997 and 2016 the count ranged from 9,428 to 27,146. Somewhere in that crowd, every returning mother picks out one voice.
method: Live pups counted on San Miguel Island, the westernmost Channel Island, between July 20 and 30 of each year, as reported by California’s Office of Environmental Health Hazard Assessment. One dot stands for 25 pups (rounded).
source: OEHHA, Indicators of Climate Change in California: sea lion pup demography | https://oehha.ca.gov/media/epic/downloads/ibs_cslpd2018.pdf
source: NOAA Fisheries: California sea lion | https://www.fisheries.noaa.gov/species/california-sea-lion
alt: Two blocks of dots, one dot per 25 pups: the lowest count, 9,428, and the highest, 27,146.


# ───────────────────────────── care ─────────────────────────────

[care.game]
title: Care: Pac-Man
text:
Mom heads out to sea and eats her way through it like Pac-Man. Her pup waits on the beach.

A mother nurses her pup for 1 to 2 days, then spends 2 to 5 days feeding at sea. While she is away, the pup does not eat. Weaning takes almost a year.
source: NOAA Fisheries: California sea lion | https://www.fisheries.noaa.gov/species/california-sea-lion
alt: Pac-Man game. A mother sea lion eats a trail of fish through a kelp maze while her pup waits on the shore.

[care.chart]
title: Mostly away
finding: Mothers spend most of their time at sea. In winter and spring their feeding trips lasted 3.3 to 4.6 days on average, and their nursing visits ashore only 1.2 to 1.4 days. In most study years they were at sea more than 70% of the time.
method: Researchers followed mothers and pups on San Miguel Island during the non-breeding seasons of 1991–1994. Each bar spans the range of the average values the study reports for different years and seasons.
source: Melin et al. 2000, Marine Mammal Science: attendance patterns at San Miguel Island | https://pubs.usgs.gov/publication/70023210
alt: Two range bars on a scale of days: at sea feeding, 3.3 to 4.6 days; ashore nursing, 1.2 to 1.4 days.


# ───────────────────────────── heal ─────────────────────────────

[heal.game]
title: Heal: Space Invaders
text:
Waves of trouble march toward the coast, and the rescue team fires back.

Scientists can sometimes trace sea lion strandings to disease, harmful algal blooms or boat strikes, but the cause often remains unknown. Getting caught in fishing gear is one of the main threats.
source: NOAA Fisheries: California sea lion | https://www.fisheries.noaa.gov/species/california-sea-lion
alt: Space Invaders game. Rows of fishing-line loops, toxic algae and germs move down; a rescue cannon shoots them one by one.

[heal.chart]
title: Hunger comes first
finding: Of 3,707 California sea lions found stranded alive on the central California coast in 1991–2000, the most common cause was malnutrition (32%), then leptospirosis, a bacterial disease (27%), trauma (18%), domoic acid poisoning from toxic algae (9%) and cancer (3%). Malnutrition strandings peaked in the El Niño years 1992, 1993 and 1998.
method: Researchers reviewed the records of 3,707 sea lions that stranded alive along the central California coast in those ten years. The "other" bar, 11%, is what is left after the five causes the study reports; that is our calculation.
source: Greig, Gulland & Kreuder 2005, Aquatic Mammals 31(1):11–22 | https://doi.org/10.1578/AM.31.1.2005.11
alt: Bar chart of stranding causes: malnutrition 32%, leptospirosis 27%, trauma 18%, domoic acid 9%, cancer 3%, other 11%.


# ───────────────────────────── heat ─────────────────────────────

[heat.game]
title: Heat: Mario run
text:
{name} dashes across a hot beach like Mario, dives in and floats with a flipper in the air.

That pose is called rafting: sea lions hold their flippers above the water, motionless, to rest and to regulate their body temperature.
source: NOAA Fisheries: California sea lion | https://www.fisheries.noaa.gov/species/california-sea-lion
alt: Platform game. A sea lion hops across sunny sand, bumps a block that drops water, jumps into the sea and rafts with a flipper raised.

[heat.chart]
title: Cold costs the young more
finding: Adult females rest comfortably in water down to about 6.4 °C, some 4 °C colder than the sea off California usually gets. Juveniles showed no comfort zone between 5 and 20 °C: their resting metabolism increased as the water approached 12 °C, so cold water costs them extra energy.
method: Researchers measured the oxygen use of 5 adult females (82–107 kg) and 4 juveniles (26–37 kg) resting in water at 0–20 °C (adults) and 5–20 °C (juveniles). The comfort zone is the "thermal neutral zone": the temperatures at which the body does not spend extra energy to stay warm. The coast marker (about 10 °C) is 6.4 °C plus the study's "about 4 °C".
source: Liwanag et al. 2009, Journal of Experimental Biology 212:3977–3984 | https://doi.org/10.1242/jeb.033282
alt: A water-temperature scale from 0 to 20 °C. The adults' comfort zone starts at 6.4 °C; the juveniles have none in the tested range.


# ───────────────────────────── tags ─────────────────────────────

[tags.game]
title: Tags: Frogger
text:
Each lane is one year of life. Can a marked pup make it across?

Since 1987, NOAA scientists have put permanent marks on pups on San Miguel Island and looked for them again, year after year.
source: DeLong et al. 2017, Marine Mammal Science 33(4):1097–1125 | https://doi.org/10.1111/mms.12427
alt: Frogger game. A marked pup hops up through five lanes of moving boats and nets, one lane for each year of age; the first lane is the busiest.

[tags.chart]
title: The first year is the hardest
finding: On average, 60% of female pups and 57% of male pups survived their first year. Yearlings did better (76% of both sexes), and survival peaked at age five: 95% of females and 93% of males lived to see the next year.
method: Mark-recapture analysis of pups branded on San Miguel Island from 1987 to 2014 and resighted from 1990 to 2015. Bars show the average annual survival at each age.
source: DeLong et al. 2017, Marine Mammal Science 33(4):1097–1125 | https://doi.org/10.1111/mms.12427
alt: Paired bars for females and males: pups 60% and 57%, yearlings 76% and 76%, five-year-olds 95% and 93%.
